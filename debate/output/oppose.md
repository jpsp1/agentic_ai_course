I oppose the motion.

**LLMs should not be strongly regulated by law because heavy-handed regulation would slow innovation, entrench big tech, and be too rigid for a fast-changing technology.** The very harms cited—misinformation, bias, privacy risks, unsafe use—are real, but they are better addressed through targeted enforcement of existing laws and flexible standards, not broad, preemptive legal controls.

First, **strong regulation would freeze a moving target**. LLMs evolve too quickly for detailed laws to keep up. By the time legislation is written, debated, and enforced, the technology will have changed. That means laws will either become outdated or so vague that they create uncertainty and over-compliance. In practice, this punishes smaller innovators far more than major firms with armies of lawyers. The result is not safety—it is market concentration.

Second, **overregulation protects incumbents**. Large corporations can absorb compliance costs, but startups, researchers, and open-source developers cannot. If we make it expensive and legally risky to build or deploy LLMs, we hand the future of AI to a handful of dominant companies. That reduces competition, slows progress, and gives the public fewer choices, not more.

Third, **the harms of LLMs are already covered by existing law**. Fraud is illegal. Defamation is illegal. Privacy violations are illegal. Discrimination is illegal. The right response is to enforce those laws against bad actors, not create sweeping new rules that treat all LLM development as presumptively dangerous. We do not ban the printing press because someone might print lies; we punish the lie, not the tool.

Finally, **innovation itself is a public good**. LLMs can expand access to education, healthcare support, coding, translation, and productivity for millions of people. Strong regulation risks delaying those benefits and making powerful tools available only to privileged institutions. A lighter-touch approach—transparency, audits where appropriate, and targeted liability for concrete harm—protects users without smothering progress.

So the choice is not between safety and chaos. It is between **smart, flexible enforcement** and **burdensome regulation that stifles innovation, entrenches monopolies, and fails to keep pace with technology**. For that reason, LLMs should not be strongly regulated by law.
